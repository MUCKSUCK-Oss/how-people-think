# Secret agencies and governments using social media ("meme wars"): starter notes
*Added 11 Nov 2026. Cases checked online through news reports and research summaries. **Balance rule:** cases from several countries, including the US and its allies, not just "enemy" countries.*

## What this is
Governments and their agencies (military, intelligence, ministries) run **fake accounts that pretend to be ordinary people**. They post memes and opinions to push a story about a country, a vaccine, a war or a person. The official name is **"coordinated inauthentic behaviour"** (Meta's term) or **"covert influence operations"**.

## Real, documented cases
| Country | Case | What happened | Source quality |
|---|---|---|---|
| 🇷🇺 Russia | **Internet Research Agency** (2016 US election) | A St Petersburg "troll factory" ran thousands of fake American accounts, pages and memes on both left and right | ✔ Very well documented (US Senate Intelligence Committee, Mueller report) |
| 🇺🇸 USA | **Pentagon anti-vaccine campaign** (2020–21) | The US military secretly ran fake Filipino accounts (at least 300 on X) attacking China's Sinovac vaccine, masks and test kits, to weaken China's influence. Reported by **Reuters, June 2024**. The Biden administration ended it in mid-2021 | ✔ A major Reuters investigation; the Pentagon partly acknowledged it, and the Philippines asked for answers |
| 🇺🇸🇬🇧 (likely) | **"Unheard Voice"** (2022) | Twitter and Meta removed a pro-Western network of fake accounts targeting the Middle East and Central Asia. Stanford Internet Observatory and Graphika called it the biggest pro-Western covert operation analysed so far. **Who ran it isn't officially confirmed** (Meta said it came from the US) | ✔ A Stanford research report |
| 🇨🇳 China | **"Spamouflage"** (2023) | Meta deleted about 7,700 Facebook accounts and nearly 1,000 Pages, linked to Chinese law enforcement; it ran on 50+ platforms. Meta called it the largest known cross-platform operation | ✔ Meta's own threat report (the "largest" label is Meta's own claim) |
| 🇨🇳 China | **"50 Cent Army"** | The government's fake posts mostly **distract** (cheerleading, changing the subject) rather than argue (King, Pan & Roberts 2017) | ✔ Already cited in the paper |
| 🇮🇱 Israel | **Stoic campaign** (2024) | The Diaspora Affairs Ministry reportedly paid about $2 million to a firm that ran hundreds of fake "American" accounts (some written with AI) targeting US lawmakers over Gaza. Meta and OpenAI confirmed and removed the network; Israel denies it | ✔ NYT, confirmed by Meta and OpenAI; officially denied |
| 🇮🇳 India | **Party "IT cells"** | Several Indian parties run organised social media teams; some have been linked to fake accounts and coordinated hashtags | **[CHECK]** Find solid studies before using this. **Cover more than one party** |

**Meme wars:** the "Great Meme War" of 2016 (Pepe memes for Trump) was mostly **grassroots**: ordinary users, not an agency. That's an important difference. Not every viral meme campaign is a government operation, and saying so would be a conspiracy-theory mistake.

## ⭐ The surprising research finding: most of these operations barely work
- **Eady et al. 2023, *Nature Communications*:** linked Twitter data for about 1,500 Americans in 2016 with surveys. Exposure to Russian troll accounts showed **no measurable effect** on attitudes, polarisation or voting.
  - Exposure was very concentrated: **1% of users saw 70%** of it, mostly people who were already strong Republicans.
  - People saw far more posts from ordinary news media and politicians than from Russian trolls.
- **Meta (Spamouflage)** and the **Stanford report (Unheard Voice)** both say these networks **struggled to reach real people**: most posts got only a handful of likes.
- 🔗 **This fits our paper perfectly:**
  - **Section 3.2:** "persuasion is often weak", because people reject messages from sources they don't trust.
  - **Simulation:** confirmation bias blocks extreme messages, and the trolls mostly reached people who already agreed.
  - **Then why do governments keep doing it?**
    - It's cheap.
    - It may work on narrow targets (the Philippine vaccine case).
    - Its real effect may be **"nobody can trust anything anymore"**, plus distraction (the 50 Cent Army).

## Simulation idea (for Step 3)
- AI people scroll a fake social media feed about one topic, such as a vaccine or a foreign country.
- **Versions:** no fake accounts; a few fake "ordinary people" posts; many fake posts; fake posts using memes vs serious text.
- **Measure:** do they change their opinion, and do they trust the posts? Does it work better on people who already lean that way?
