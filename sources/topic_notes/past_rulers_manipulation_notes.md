# How rulers in the past steered what people believed: cases for Section 2.5 ("Then and now")
*Overnight research, 16 Nov 2026. Each case gives: what happened, **what historians agree on** (and what is disputed), the **technique**, and the **modern equivalent**. ## 1. Ramesses II and the Battle of Kadesh (Egypt, about 1274 BC): the oldest "we won" spin
- **What happened:** Ramesses II fought the Hittites at Kadesh. His temple walls and the "Poem of Pentaur" show him turning the battle almost alone, like a god.
- **What historians agree on:** the battle was a **stalemate**. Kadesh stayed under Hittite control. The Hittite king also claimed victory. About 15 years later the two sides signed one of the **earliest known peace treaties** (about 1259 BC), and its terms favoured the Hittites [summary: World History Encyclopedia; History Skills; Milestone Documents].
- **Technique:** claim victory, carve it in stone (permanent and repeated), and make the ruler the hero.
- **Modern equivalent:** both sides announcing victory after a ceasefire; official war films that show only the winning moments (see the movies RESEARCH.md, Part F).

## 2. Egypt's pyramids: monuments that bind labour and loyalty (about 2500 BC)
- **What historians agree on:** the Great Pyramid was **Khufu's tomb**. It was built by **paid or conscripted workers**, not slaves, who lived in a workers' town at Giza (Lehner, Hawass). The pharaoh was presented as divine [summary; widely documented].
- **Disputed:** Predictive History claims it was not a tomb and calls "power plant" ideas plausible. ❌ This is against the expert consensus (see the YouTube notes, 10c).
- **Technique:** giant shared projects plus god-king ideology, so work for the ruler feels like worship.
- **Modern equivalent:** prestige megaprojects and national monuments used as proof of a leader's greatness.

## 3. Ashoka's edicts (India, 3rd century BC): one message, edited for each audience
- **What happened:** Ashoka had his messages (on *dhamma*, tolerance and good government) carved on rocks and pillars across the empire. **Rock Edict 13** expresses **remorse** for the slaughter in his conquest of Kalinga.
- **What historians agree on:** in **Kalinga itself** (at Dhauli and Jaugada) Edicts 11–13, **including the remorse edict**, were left out. Two "separate edicts" took their place, addressed to local officials about fair justice [summary: Major Rock Edicts sources; scholarly editions such as Hultzsch; CHECK against a scholarly edition before quoting]. Scholars debate why. One common reading is that a public apology was thought unwise among the very people he had conquered.
- **Technique:** **the same ruler tailors the message by region**. He shows regret where it earns moral prestige and order where it keeps control.
- **Modern equivalent:** targeted political ads, a different message for each audience (paper Section 6.6, personal targeting). Carved stone was the "broadcast medium" of its day.
- **Balance note:** Ashoka is admired, rightly, for tolerance. Persuasion is not always sinister; the point is that **even admired rulers managed their image**.

## 4. Qin and Han China: who controls the books controls the past (221 BC onwards)
- **What happened:** the First Emperor of Qin ordered books burned (213 BC). The Han historian Sima Qian also says he had scholars buried alive (212 BC).
- **What historians agree on:** **book destruction did happen** in some form; Qin gathered and destroyed works it saw as subversive. The **"burying the scholars"** story is **doubted**. It was written about a century later by an official of the Han, the dynasty that replaced Qin and had reasons to blacken it. Scholars such as Michael Nylan and Li Kaiyuan argue it is partly legend [summary: Wikipedia, "Burning of books and burying of scholars", which cites Nylan, Kern and Li; UCLA lecture notes].
- **The double lesson:** (1) Qin controlled ideas by destroying them; (2) **Han controlled the memory of Qin** by writing its history. Each Chinese dynasty wrote the official history of the one before it.
- **Modern equivalent:** state-approved textbooks; deleting posts and accounts; and "the winners' history", where a new government rewrites how the old one is remembered.

## 5. Imperial China's exams: keeping the elite divided (Song to Qing, about 1000–1905)
- **What happened:** officials were chosen by written exams on the Confucian classics.
- **What scholars argue:** political scientist **Yuhua Wang** (Harvard, *The Rise and Fall of Imperial China*, 2022) argues that emperors faced a "sovereign's dilemma": a united elite can make the state strong but can also overthrow the ruler. The exams helped **break up the old aristocratic networks**, so elites competed for the emperor's favour. Emperors stayed in power longer, but the state became weaker [summary: Princeton UP; Fairbank Center]. Reviewers question how far the model reaches beyond China [summary].
- **Example:** in **1397** all 51 passing candidates were southerners. The Ming founder punished the officials involved, executing one investigator and exiling the chief examiner, and issued an all-northern list; regional quotas came later, in 1425 [summary: The World of Chinese]. (Predictive History says he "killed them all": ⚠️ exaggerated.)
- **Technique:** control people's **ambitions**: make the one ladder to status run through the ruler's own test.
- **Modern equivalent:** coaching-centre culture and high-stakes exams as the only "fair" route (paper Section 4.8, the belief that the race is fair; Section 6.4).

## 6. Augustus (Rome, 27 BC–AD 14): keep the old labels, change the substance
- **What historians agree on:** Augustus said he had **"restored the Republic"** and handed power back to the Senate (27 BC), while he controlled the armies, provinces and treasury. His own account, the ***Res Gestae***, was set up in bronze at his tomb and copied across the empire. It presents him as a servant of the Republic and **never names Mark Antony**. Coins called him *Divi filius* ("son of the deified one"). Monuments like the **Ara Pacis** linked his rule with peace and plenty [summary: Open University "The many guises of the emperor Augustus"; Paul Zanker, *The Power of Images in the Age of Augustus*; Brunt & Moore's commentary].
- **Technique:** leaving things out (Antony disappears), using official media (coins, monuments, inscriptions) as **repetition**, and keeping familiar names so the change feels safe (Le Bon, Section 2.1; Machiavelli, Section 2.4).
- **Modern equivalent:** leaders who keep elections, courts and parliaments in name while emptying them out; official biographies.

## 7. The Mughal court: painting the emperor as master of the world (1600s)
- **What happened:** around 1618 the court artist **Abu'l Hasan** painted ***Jahangir Embracing Shah Abbas***. The two rulers **never met**; Jahangir reportedly dreamed it. Jahangir stands on a lion and towers over the Persian Shah, who stands on a lamb, both on a globe [summary: Smithsonian Freer Gallery; Wikipedia].
- **What historians agree on:** the Smithsonian says the artist "manipulated symbols of sovereignty" to proclaim the emperor's superiority. Such allegorical portraits began under Akbar and grew under Jahangir. Akbar's court chronicle, the *Akbarnama*, also presented his rule as divinely blessed [summary; CHECK the *Akbarnama* point with a scholarly source].
- **Technique:** **imaginary events shown as real**, plus symbols that make one ruler look bigger.
- **Modern equivalent:** staged photo-ops, edited images, AI-generated images of leaders.
- **Balance note:** this is court art, made for a small elite audience, not mass propaganda. The audience size matters.

## 8. Napoleon (France, 1796–1815): the general who made his own myth
- **What historians agree on:**
  - His army **bulletins** were written or dictated by Napoleon himself and so unreliable that soldiers said "to lie like a bulletin" [summary: napoleon.org; Markham, *Imperial Glory*].
  - Gros's *Bonaparte at the Pont d'Arcole* is called the first image of the Napoleonic myth. David's *Napoleon Crossing the Alps* is "not in any way historically accurate" (he actually crossed on a mule) [summary: Open University; napoleon.org].
  - He closed most Paris newspapers and kept tight control of the rest [summary; CHECK the exact number by 1810].
- **The revolution behind him:** the officer corps went from about 90% noble before 1789 to about 3% by 1794, which opened careers to talent [summary: see the YouTube notes, 10a].
- **Technique:** a **hero image** in art and news, **controlling the channels**, and **war as a stage** that keeps the myth alive (Predictive History's claim; plausible but interpretive).
- **Modern equivalent:** leaders' personal media teams, choreographed rallies, and the Pentagon/Hollywood pattern of the heroic military image.

## 9. Colonial India: the press as a threat to be licensed (1878–1910)
- **What historians agree on:** the **Vernacular Press Act (1878)**, under Viceroy Lytton, applied **only to Indian-language newspapers**; English papers were exempt. Papers could avoid penalties by sending content to a government censor, and there was no appeal to the courts. Critics called it the "Gagging Act"; it was repealed in 1882. The **Indian Press Act (1910)** let the government demand large security deposits and seize presses [summary: Wikipedia; Indian Express via iPleaders].
- **A detail worth using:** *Amrita Bazar Patrika* reportedly **switched from Bengali to English overnight** to escape the Act [summary; CHECK].
- **Technique:** **censor the language the masses read**, and leave the elite's language freer.
- **Modern equivalent:** internet shutdowns and blocking orders that hit regional or mass platforms; laws aimed at "fake news" written broadly; licence and NOC systems (see the movies RESEARCH.md, F4).

## 10. Stalin's USSR: removing people from photographs (1930s)
- **What historians agree on:** NKVD chief **Nikolai Yezhov** was photographed walking with Stalin along the Moscow–Volga canal (1937). After his fall and execution (1940; sources differ on the year), he was **retouched out**; the censors painted water where he had stood. David King's book ***The Commissar Vanishes*** (1997) collects many such cases [summary: Hoover Institution; History.com; Newseum].
- **Technique:** **leaving things out**, here literally erasing a person.
- **Modern equivalent:** deleted tweets and pages, "memory-holing", and now AI image editing. Our movies simulation found that revealing a *cut scene* hurt trust more than revealing approval (−0.44).

## 11. Nazi Germany: radio, with real causal evidence (1930s)
- **What the best study found:** Adena, Enikolopov, Petrova, Santarosa & Zhuravskaya, ***Quarterly Journal of Economics*** (2015), compared German areas with and without radio reception:
  - while radio carried pro-government (Weimar) news, it **slowed** the Nazis' growth;
  - once the Nazis controlled it, radio **helped** them win support and members, and encouraged denunciations of Jews;
  - Nazi radio worked **best where antisemitism was already high**, and **backfired** where it was historically low [summary: QJE abstract; SSRN].
- **Content warning:** hate propaganda, described only as history.
- **Why it matters for the paper:** like Yao 2024 for China's films, propaganda **amplifies what people already believe** and can backfire on those who don't. That matches the paper's simulation finding that strong prior views resist (Sections 5.6, 6.11).
- **Modern equivalent:** algorithmic feeds that deliver identity-confirming content to people already inclined to believe it.

## Summary table for Section 2.5
| Then | Technique | Now |
|---|---|---|
| Ramesses at Kadesh | Claim victory, carve it in stone | Both sides "win" every war in their own media |
| Ashoka's edited edicts | Same ruler, different message by region | Targeted ads (6.6) |
| Qin book burning / Han history | Destroy texts; winners write history | Deleting posts; textbook rewrites |
| Chinese exams | One ladder to status, run by the ruler | Exam-and-coaching race (6.4, 4.8) |
| Augustus | Keep old labels, hollow out substance; leave out rivals | Elections kept in name only; official biographies |
| Mughal allegories | Show imagined events as real | Photo-ops, AI images |
| Napoleon | Self-written bulletins, hero paintings, press control | Personal media teams, rallies, hero films (6.11) |
| Vernacular Press Act | Censor the masses' language | Shutdowns, vague "fake news" laws, NOCs |
| Stalin's photos | Erase people from the record | Deleted posts, memory-holing |
| Nazi radio (Adena 2015) | New mass medium + prior hatred | Algorithmic feeds |

**Pattern across 3,000 years (my reading, for Maulik to judge):** the *media* change (stone, paintings, newspapers, radio, film, feeds), but the moves stay the same: **leave things out, repeat the hero story, control the channel, and tailor the message**. The best causal studies (Adena 2015 for radio; Yao 2024 for film) agree on one thing: **propaganda mostly strengthens what people already lean towards and can backfire on sceptics.** That fits Section 3.2 ("persuasion is often weak").
