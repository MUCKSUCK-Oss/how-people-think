## 1. Philosophers don't agree on one line
The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on the ethics of manipulation [read, about 85% of the page] sorts the views into three families, plus attempts to join them:

| Family | The test | Who | A problem with it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hidden influence** | Does it work **without you noticing**, and could you not easily notice? | Susser, Roessler & Nissenbaum 2019 ("manipulation is hidden influence"); Ware 1981 | Guilt trips and nagging are manipulative but not hidden |
| **Hidden aim** | Is the persuader **hiding what they really want**? | Belohrad 2019; Whitfield 2020 | A nagging child's aim is obvious |
| **Bypassing reason** | Does it work **around your thinking instead of through it**? | Raz 1988; Gorin 2018 ("fails to track reasons"); Sunstein 2016 ("fails to sufficiently engage" deliberation) | A graphic cigarette warning works on feelings but seems fair |
| **Trickery** | Does it leave you with a **false or bad belief, desire or feeling**, i.e. one that falls short of how a good thinker would form it? | Noggle 1996; Barnhill 2014; Mills 1995 ("bad reasons dressed as good ones") | Framing with true facts ("90% survive" vs "10% die") is hard to judge |
| **Pressure** | Does it push through **pressure instead of good-faith argument** (between persuading and forcing)? | Faden, Beauchamp & King 1986; Kligman & Culver 1992 | Where does a strong plea become pressure? |
| **Joined up** | Manipulation is a **mistake the influencer tries to cause**: a wrong belief (trickery) or acting against your own better judgement (pressure) | Noggle 2025 | Still debated |
| **No clean line** | It's a **cluster** idea; a rough definition is enough for law | Cohen 2025; Sunstein 2025 | — |

**Other points from the entry:**
- **Is it always wrong?** Most philosophers say **no, but there is always a reason against it** that can be outweighed. Their example is tricking a terrorist into revealing where a bomb is. (Compare our movies "rules work" film.)
- **Can it use true facts?** Yes. Choosing *which* true fact to show, or framing it ("survival" vs "death"), can manipulate.
- **Does it need intent?** Views differ. One example is a person who manipulates relatives with gifts **without meaning to**. With algorithms, some say intent isn't needed at all.

## 2. What law says (it uses effects, not intentions)
- **India, CCPA Dark Patterns Guidelines (30 Nov 2023)** [summary]: dark patterns are design practices that **mislead or trick users into doing something they did not originally intend or want**, by weakening their autonomy or choice. 13 named types (false urgency, basket sneaking, confirm-shaming, nagging, subscription traps…). The RBI adds rules for financial products from 1 Jan 2027 [summary].
- **EU AI Act, Article 5(1)(a)** [summary: Commission page + commentary]: bans AI that uses **subliminal, purposefully manipulative or deceptive techniques** that **"materially distort"** behaviour (appreciably weakening a person's ability to make an informed decision) and are likely to cause **significant harm**.
  - Intent is not required.
  - "Lawful persuasion" and mild nudges are outside it.
  - An example in the Commission's guidelines is a chatbot that copies a relative's voice.
- **What both laws have in common:** they judge by **effect on your choice and harm**, not by what the company meant.

## 3. Rules from the people who invented "nudges"
- **Thaler & Sunstein** [summary: Thaler's NYT column]: a nudge is acceptable if it is
  1. **transparent and never misleading**;
  2. **easy to opt out of** (ideally one click);
  3. **likely to make the nudged person better off**.

  Breaking these turns a nudge into **"sludge"**.
- **Rawls's publicity test** (used in nudge debates) [summary]: would the method still work if **everyone knew it was being used**? If it only works in secret, be suspicious.
- **Tristan Harris** (already in the paper): persuasion is ethical only when **the persuader's goals match yours**.
- **Cialdini** (already in the paper): the same six principles are ethical when they point to **true** facts, manipulative when **faked**.

## 4. What ordinary people think (survey)
- **Reisch & Sunstein 2016**, *Judgment and Decision Making* 11(4) [summary]: nationally representative surveys in Denmark, France, Germany, Hungary, Italy and the UK.
  - Most people **supported 12 of 15 nudges**.
  - They **rejected a subliminal anti-smoking ad** in cinemas, even though its goal was good.
  - So people seem to care about **how** (hidden or open), not only **why**.

## 5. How this connects to our own results
- **Hidden vs open:** the "ministry approved this" notice (movies), "a foreign government ran these accounts" (secret agencies) and honest disclosure (universities, healthcare) all **revealed a hidden influence**, and the influence mostly stopped working. That fits the hidden-influence family (Susser) and Rawls's publicity test.
- **But noticing isn't enough:** AI people called every film propaganda and were still moved by the "rules work" film; many called an ad a lie and then followed the neighbour's story. So "it's fine if people *could* notice" may be too weak a test.
- **Pressure without lies:** phone-only cancelling (corporations) told no lies and hid nothing, yet kept 42% paying. That fits the pressure family better than the trickery family.
- **True facts can still manipulate:** "9 in 10 lose money" (FOMO) and framing in Section 4.4.
