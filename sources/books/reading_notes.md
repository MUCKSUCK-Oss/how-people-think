## 1. Gustave Le Bon, *The Crowd* (1895)
**Read:** Book II, Chapter III, sections 2–3, "The Means of Action of the Leaders" and "Prestige" (file lines 3744–3960).

**Key ideas (my words):**
- To plant a belief in a crowd, leaders use three tools: **affirmation** (state it boldly, with no proof), **repetition** (say it again and again in the same words), and **contagion** (once enough people repeat it, it spreads like a germ).
- Short claims with *no* proof work better than argued ones.
- Repetition works partly because we **forget where we heard it**. After seeing "X's chocolate is the best" a thousand times, we think we've heard it from many people. Modern psychology calls this *source confusion*, and it's part of the illusory truth effect.
- Only an opposite repetition can fight a repetition: "affirmation and repetition are alone powerful enough to combat each other". That's a real insight into why fact-checks are weak (simulation Figure 2).
- People imitate a few individuals, but only ones not *too* far from current ideas. Someone too far ahead "has no influence".
- **Prestige** is a kind of domination that "paralyses our critical faculty". It's the root of all authority.

**Short quote:** "Affirmation pure and simple, kept free of all reasoning and all proof, is one of the surest means of making an idea enter the mind of crowds."

**Links to your casebook:** Trump's short repeated slogans; Jiang's confident, proof-free predictions (Report 4); Ruchir's "feeling of fair opportunity" spreading by contagion.

⚠ **Be careful:** Le Bon also says prejudiced things about women, workers and "Eastern people", and his "crowd madness" idea is now seen as exaggerated. Later researchers (e.g. Reicher's work on crowds) show crowds often act rationally. Use him for the tactics, and criticise him in your paper.

### New in version 2: all of Book I (lines 559–2279)
**What a "crowd" is:**
- Not people standing in one place. A whole nation can become a "psychological crowd" during a big emotional event.
- 🔗 Today, a trending topic on social media is the closest thing.

**Three reasons people act differently in a crowd:**
1. **Anonymity:** nobody feels responsible.
2. **Contagion:** feelings spread.
3. **Suggestibility.**
- He compares the third to hypnosis. That science is outdated, but the observation still holds: juries and parliaments sometimes vote for things each member alone would reject.

**Educated people are fooled too (ch. II):**
- A whole ship's crew, officers included, "saw" a raft of desperate sailors. It turned out to be floating tree branches.
- A man faked a séance for a group of distinguished scientists, and their written reports said only supernatural powers could explain it.
- His point: in a crowd, "the learned man and the ignoramus" observe equally badly.
- 🔗 This matches the simulation's finding that careful thinkers lose most of their advantage under pressure. It also matches Lippmann's staged-fight experiment.

**Many witnesses agreeing is weak evidence:**
- Usually the first witness's mistake spreads to the rest.
- 🔗 Modern research on memory conformity and Asch's experiments support this.

**How a speaker moves a crowd:** exaggerate, affirm, repeat, and never try to prove anything. He lists this as a standard method of public speakers. 🔗 Report 1 (simple repeated slogans) and Report 4 (Jiang's soundbite habit).

**Crowds and power:**
- Crowds respect strength and see kindness as weakness. Their favourite hero "will always have the semblance of a Caesar".
- 🔗 Jiang compares Trump to Caesar (Report 1, Part F).
- Le Bon also says crowds are deeply conservative under their noisy outbursts: they change the names of institutions more than the institutions themselves.

**Ideas must be simple images (ch. III):**
- An idea's influence has nothing to do with whether it's true.
- Contradictory ideas can sit side by side in one mind.
- Even a convinced person slides back to their old view within days, because old beliefs have become feelings.
- 🔗 Belief perseverance and the continued-influence effect. This is the reason the simulation's fact-check removes only part of the effect.

**One image beats a thousand statistics:**
- A flu epidemic killing 5,000 Parisians barely registered, because it arrived as weekly numbers. One dramatic public accident killing 500 would have shaken everyone.
- 🔗 The identifiable victim effect and "psychic numbing" (Slovic). **A strong idea for simulation v3:** a vivid story vs a statistic.

**Napoleon adapted to his audience's beliefs:** Le Bon reports him saying he became a Catholic to end one war and a Muslim to gain a footing in Egypt. 🔗 A politician's identity shifting by audience.

**Convictions take a religious shape (ch. IV):**
- Followers worship a leader or idea, fear its power, obey without discussion, want to spread it, and see non-believers as enemies.
- He says this applies to political creeds and heroes as much as to gods. His example is General Boulanger, whose portrait hung in every country inn.
- 🔗 Report 4, Part F ("nationalism and cults: little difference") and political fan culture today.

⚠ **More cautions from Book I:**
- He calls women, children and "savages" inferior thinkers, and ranks "Latin" crowds by race.
- He says children "invariably lie".
- These are prejudices of his time, not findings. Quote the mechanism, never the prejudice.

## 2. Edward Bernays, *Propaganda* (1928)
**Read:** Chapter I "Organizing Chaos" (lines 112–356) and Chapter IV "The Psychology of Public Relations" (lines 1021–1382).

**Key ideas:**
- Bernays was a real manipulator (he was Freud's nephew and is called "the father of PR"), and he *openly* explains the method. He says an "invisible government" of a few people who understand mass psychology shapes our tastes and ideas, and he thinks this is **necessary** for democracy to work. A very "oh, they are doing this" book.
- **We can't check everything, so we let others narrow our choices.** Parties cut the field to 2–3 candidates; ads cut the dozens of soaps to a few. This is exactly where defaults and decoys get their power.
- **People don't know their real reasons.** A man thinks he chose a car by studying it, but really a friend bought one, or it shows status. The propagandist must find the hidden motive.
- **Use trusted authorities instead of ads.** To sell more bacon, don't shout "eat bacon": get *doctors* to say bacon is healthy, because people obey their doctors. 🔗 This links directly to your **pharma report** (doctors as the target of drug marketing).
- **Make the buyer ask for it.** To sell pianos, don't advertise pianos. Make "a music room" fashionable through famous people and architects. Then buying a piano "will come to him as his own idea".
- **Clichés steer emotion.** Labelling someone with a scary word ("Bolshevik") stampedes voters. Renaming "hospital" to "evacuation post" made criticism vanish. This is framing.
- **Get schools on your side.** Ivory soap ran national soap-carving competitions in schools, so children and teachers promoted the brand for free. 🔗 Links to your **education report**.

**Short quote:** "It will come to him as his own idea."

**Your opinion questions:** Is Bernays right that some "invisible government" is unavoidable? Where is the line between helping people choose and manipulating them?

⚠ **Copyright note:** Bernays died in 1995. The book is public domain in the USA (since 2024) but probably still under copyright in India, where it lasts until 60 years after the author's death. Use the file for personal study only, and cite it normally.

### New in version 2: chapters II, III, V, VI, VIII, XI
Read: lines 357–1020 and 1383–2687, 2842–3170, 3531–3764. **Not read:** ch. VII (women's activities), IX (social service), X (art and science).

**Ch. II, The New Propaganda:**
- "Propaganda is the executive arm of the invisible government."
- He says universal literacy didn't give people independent minds. It gave them "rubber stamps": the same slogans as millions of others.
- **The new method he learned in WWI:** don't persuade individuals; win the "key men" of each group (clubs, churches, unions), and their members follow. 🔗 Today's influencer marketing.
- **Case: velvet.** Manufacturers got Paris designers and duchesses to wear velvet. US magazines reported it as a natural trend, and stores copied each other. "A demand was… deliberately created." 🔗 An "organic" trend that was manufactured.

**Ch. III, The New Propagandists:**
- Even leaders are led. A congressman follows a boss nobody knows; a famous preacher takes his ideas from higher up.
- **Case: the gentleman tailor.** An anonymous London tailor chooses next season's suit for British nobles; a US firm copies it; fashionable city men wear it; then a man in Topeka wears it, believing he chose it himself. 🔗 A chain of imitation, the "two-step flow" (Katz & Lazarsfeld 1955).
- **Case: silk shoes.** A silk company got a shoe company to cover shoes in silk and an actress to wear them. 🔗 Celebrity endorsement.
- He claims PR people refuse dishonest clients and always label their material. Compare that with the cigarette campaigns he later ran (see Tye, *The Father of Spin*, 1998 **[CHECK]**).

**Ch. V, Business and the Public:**
- **Why propaganda grew:** mass production means "supply must actively seek to create its corresponding demand". A factory can't wait for people to want its product.
- **Two methods:**
  - **"continuous interpretation":** control every point of contact so people get the desired impression, "often without being conscious of it";
  - **"high-spotting":** one dramatic stunt that stands for the whole company, like making a skyscraper ten feet taller than the tallest one.
  - 🔗 Bryan Johnson's prediction-market stunt (Report 2) is high-spotting; Mankind's logo held on screen (Report 2) is continuous interpretation.
- **The indirect route:**
  - The luggage industry aimed its campaign at railways and steamship lines, not at buyers.
  - A shoe maker set up a "foot protection bureau" that put out accurate foot-care advice, so police and fire chiefs promoted his shoes.
  - 🔗 Industry-funded "information bureaus" and health institutes today, and pharma reaching patients through doctors.
- **Stage an event instead of denying a rumour:** when rumours said Beech-Nut would sell and move, the town held a "Beech-Nut Day" honouring the owner's loyalty. He notes that a denial alone would have convinced fewer people.

**Ch. VI, Propaganda and Political Leadership:**
- Politicians should copy business: study the public scientifically and sell the platform like a product.
- **Personality sells a whole party.** "A charming candidate is the alchemist's secret…" He also warns that personality is overused.
- **Don't argue; create circumstances.** Instead of telling voters a tariff raises prices, stage exhibitions in 20 cities and have prominent men wear cotton suits to boycott wool. By the time the candidate speaks, the public is already asking the question. 🔗 Agenda setting; Jiang's "attention" theme (Report 4).
- **Timing for the press:** Masaryk moved Czechoslovakia's independence announcement from a Sunday to a Monday, when newspapers had more space.
- **The trial balloon:** leak an idea anonymously and watch the reaction. 🔗 Trump's tariff posts, then quiet negotiation (Report 1, item 9).
- His conclusion: "Ours must be a leadership democracy administered by the intelligent minority…" **[YOUR OPINION]** This is the sentence to argue with in your paper.

**Ch. VIII, Propaganda for Education:**
- Universities need PR too. By 1928 Harvard had a "Secretary for Information", in practice a publicity man.
- Privately funded universities depend on businessmen whose goals may conflict with scholarship.
- He admits propaganda can "overadvertise an institution and… create in the public mind artificial values".
- 🔗 Report 5 (education) and the paused universities research: rankings, donors, prestige.

**Ch. XI, The Mechanics of Propaganda:**
- The motion picture is "the greatest unconscious carrier of propaganda in the world". It reflects and exaggerates popular trends. 🔗 Report 4 (Bollywood and the police; the Hollywood–Pentagon link).
- A company may supply schools with teaching material, as long as it's accurate and the school chooses on merit. 🔗 Ivory soap carving, and today's sponsored school content.
- **His prediction:** even when the public sees the methods, propaganda won't die out; it will just make smarter appeals.
- 🔗 This is a striking match with the simulation. **Prebunking (knowing the tricks) helped only a little (87% → 85%).**

### New in version 3: chapters VII, IX and X (read 6 Nov 2026, ~2:50 am; lines 2688–2841 and 3171–3530)
**With these, all 11 chapters of *Propaganda* have now been read.**

**Ch. VII, Women's Activities and Propaganda:**
- Bernays praises women's organisations as the best users of "the new propaganda". These include the League of Women Voters and the 13,000 women's clubs. He credits them with laws such as the eight-hour day for women and maternity welfare funding.
- **The best example: the price-tag shop.** To fight a tariff, a women's consumer committee rented an empty shop in New York. They tagged every item with two prices: today's, and the price after the tariff. Hundreds of visitors joined the cause.
  - 🔗 **Psychology:** a concrete, personal comparison beats an abstract argument (vividness; the "identifiable" effect). It's the same idea as Le Bon's "one image beats statistics". Today's version is "this policy will cost *your* family ₹X a month".
- ⚠ **Caution:** he dismisses women politicians of the time (one is called a "catspaw" for her husband) and says women should handle what "men are likely to ignore". These are 1928 attitudes. Don't quote them as fact.
- **Balance point:** here propaganda serves a *minority* cause that many would call good. The tools are neutral, and the goal decides the ethics (Harris's test, paper Section 1.1).

**Ch. IX, Propaganda in Social Service:**
- "The great enemy of any attempt to change men's habits is inertia." Every health campaign (brushing teeth, TB, infant care) is propaganda in his sense: "Social service, in fact, is identical with propaganda in many cases."
- **The Atlanta conference (NAACP, anti-lynching campaign).** The strongest case in the chapter:
  1. Hold the conference **in the South** (Atlanta), because a view on a southern question has more authority coming from the South.
  2. Put **southern white ministers** on the same platform as Black leaders, saying the same thing.
  3. Result: southern editors began treating the issue as "discussable" rather than purely emotional. Bernays carefully says the drop in lynching is "very probably" partly due to this and other efforts; it can't be measured exactly.
  - 🔗 **Psychology:** this is the **"in-group messenger"** idea. A message lands best when it comes from someone the audience sees as one of their own. It links to the simulation's confirmation-bias result: people ignore views too far from their own, so you send a messenger who is *close* to them. The paper review (fix 2) found that moderate influencers get past confirmation bias better than extreme ones. Bernays did this on purpose in the 1920s.
  - 🔗 Modern parallel: health campaigns that use local religious leaders or doctors from the same community. **[CHECK]**: find one study on "trusted messenger" effects (e.g. vaccine messages from doctors of the same political side) before citing.
- He also says that while kings and priests once shaped opinion, today "the privilege of attempting to sway public opinion is every one's". This is his democratic defence of propaganda.
- **[YOUR OPINION]** If the same technique fought lynching *and* sold bacon, is the technique good, bad or neutral?

**Ch. X, Art and Science:**
- **Borrowing prestige.** American Cheney silks became fashionable after being shown at the Louvre with a French minister as patron and a famous French ironworker's designs. Department stores across the US then asked for the exhibition.
  - 🔗 The **halo effect** and authority transfer. Today: "as seen in Paris/Milan", celebrity collaborations and "Made in Japan" style labels.
- **The modernist piano.** A piano maker paid artists to design modernist pianos, expecting to sell few. The point: "People at tea parties will not talk about pianos; but they may talk about the new modernist piano."
  - 🔗 A **conversation piece** or "halo product" made only to get attention for the whole brand. Today: concept cars and limited "drops".
- **Corporate science.** He says big companies funding research should give schools and universities "the exact truth" about science, and that they are "under obligation to do so".
  - ⚠ The obvious risk is a company deciding what "the truth" is about its own products. 🔗 Report 2 and the sugar case (Kearns et al. 2016): the Sugar Research Foundation funded Harvard scientists in the 1960s. Bernays's ideal and what actually happened.
- Museums should stop being "morgues" and push beauty out to the people, e.g. by copying a Greek vase onto cheap china.

**Overall view of the book, now complete:**
- Bernays's method can be summed up as: **(1) find the group leaders, (2) borrow their authority, (3) create an event that becomes news, (4) let people feel the idea is their own.**
- He used it for bacon, pianos and silk, *and* for anti-lynching campaigns and public health. That's why the book is useful for the paper: it shows the technique has no built-in morality.

## 3. Walter Lippmann, *Public Opinion* (1922)
**Read:** Chapter I "The World Outside and the Pictures in Our Heads" (lines 184–313) and the start of Chapter VI "Stereotypes" (lines 2148–2267).

**Key ideas:**
- In 1914, a few English, French and Germans on a remote island stayed friends for six weeks after their countries went to war, because the news hadn't reached them. We all act on the **picture in our heads**, not the world as it is.
- We know most of the world only **second-hand**, through reports. Whoever controls the reports controls the picture.
- Public figures are known through a **constructed personality**, not the real person. 🔗 Links to Jiang's "Trump as myth-maker" (Report 1, Part F).
- **"We do not first see, and then define, we define first and then see."** Our culture gives us stereotypes, and we notice only the facts that fit them.
- An experiment at a psychology congress: a staged 20-second fight. Of 40 trained observers, only 1 reported it with less than 20% errors, and many invented details. Eyewitnesses aren't reliable.

**Short quote:** "For the most part we do not first see, and then define, we define first and then see."

**Links to the simulation:** echo chambers (people hear what fits their picture). Possible v2 idea: give people a "stereotype filter" that ignores information against their belief (confirmation bias).

⚠ **Copyright note:** Lippmann died in 1974, so the same India caution applies as for Bernays. Also, the Göttingen example uses racial language of its time. Don't quote that part.

## 4. Niccolò Machiavelli, *The Prince* (1532)
**Read:** Chapter XVIII "Concerning the Way in Which Princes Should Keep Faith" (lines 2510–2640).

**Key ideas:**
- A ruler must be both **lion** (force) and **fox** (cunning, to spot traps).
- Keep promises only when it helps you, since "there will never be wanting… legitimate reasons" to excuse breaking them.
- **It's not necessary to *be* good, only to *appear* good.** Above all, appear religious.
- **Most people judge by the eye, not the hand.** Everyone sees what you seem to be, and few know what you are. Those few won't oppose the majority.
- If a ruler succeeds, people will call the means honest: "the vulgar are always taken by what a thing seems to be and by what comes of it".

**Short quote:** "Every one sees what you appear to be, few really know what you are."

**Links:** Report 3 (how powerful people think); Jiang's Machiavelli-style "game theory" view of leaders; impression management in modern politics. A good counterweight: is Machiavelli *describing* rulers or *advising* them? Use this for your opinion section.

## 5. Plato, *The Republic*, Book VII: the cave (~375 BC)
**Read:** the opening of Book VII (lines 3707–3800). ⚠ This Gutenberg file starts with Benjamin Jowett's long *introduction and summary*, and the passage I read is his summary of the cave. For quotes in the paper, use the actual dialogue later in the same file, or another translation.

**Key ideas:**
- Prisoners chained since childhood see only **shadows** of puppets carried in front of a fire, and they think the shadows are reality.
- Someone freed and shown the real world is at first dazzled and wants to go back to the shadows. **Truth is painful at first.**
- If the freed person returns and tries to free others, they laugh at him and would kill him if they could.
- Education doesn't "put sight into blind eyes". It **turns the soul** toward the light.

**Links:** media and echo chambers (the shadows are what the "puppet-carriers" choose to show); Report 5 (education as turning, not filling). Jiang also uses this allegory (Report 4), so you can compare Plato's meaning with his use of it.
