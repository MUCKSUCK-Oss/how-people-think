# Research gaps, conflicts, and your original contribution

## Gaps seen so far (from a first pass; confirm with deeper search)
1. **Attention displacement beyond one leader.** Lewandowsky et al. use two outlets and the first two years of one president. The Epstein preprint is also one leader and is not peer reviewed. I did not find tests across leaders, parties or countries.
2. **Attention vs belief.** Most effect studies measure votes or attitudes. Distraction studies measure coverage. Whether distraction changes what the public cares about is largely unmeasured.
3. **Institutions.** Universities, NGOs and museums appear mostly in reporting and commentary (Columbia rankings, Kony 2012, Sackler gifts). I found no causal studies.
4. **Lab to world.** Illusory truth is demonstrated in labs; effects on real votes or purchases are not established.
5. **Satire and comedy.** No verified peer-reviewed evidence found yet.

## Conflicts
- Weak effects (Kalla & Broockman; Coppock et al.; Mercier) vs strong effects (Adena et al.; Yanagizawa-Drott; Peisakhin & Rozenas). Possible reconciliation in 03 section 3.
- Trump-tweets diversion: Lewandowsky et al. find evidence; other work cited in the diversion literature concludes diversion was limited. I have not read that other work, so find it before you cite the disagreement.

## Your original contribution: a small attention-displacement test
**Question:** After a big unrelated event, does public attention to a running controversy fall faster than it normally would?

**Design sketch**
1. Choose 8 to 12 controversies from different countries and different parts of the political spectrum, plus some non-political ones (company, university). Write the selection rule BEFORE looking at any data (for example: top controversy in a country's news during a given month, chosen by a fixed source).
2. Define a "displacing event" objectively (for example: a news story that outranks the controversy in overall search interest or Wikipedia views within 7 days).
3. Outcome: weekly or daily search interest (Google Trends, a relative index) and Wikipedia page views for the controversy's topic.
4. Compare the drop after the event with the drop after other, similar-aged controversies without an event (a control group of "ordinary decay").
5. Preregister: write what result supports and what result refutes your hypothesis before analysis. A null result is a valid finding.

**Honest limits to state in the paper:** Google Trends is relative and sampled; stories fade on their own; events may be correlated with the controversy; the test shows association, not intent; small sample means descriptive, not conclusive.

**Skills you'd show:** data collection, a pre-stated hypothesis, a control comparison, and honest limits.
