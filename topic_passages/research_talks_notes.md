# Research-based talks on persuasion and manipulation
Read on 6 Nov 2026 via the YouTube transcript panel.

**Why these talks:** the speakers are researchers or insiders whose claims link to published work, unlike opinion channels. They're good to cite next to the podcast cases.

**Balance note:** Harris is a tech critic; Cialdini wrote the classic persuasion textbook; Haidt is a centrist social psychologist who studies left and right.

---

## 1. Tristan Harris, "How a handful of tech companies control billions of minds every day" (TED 2017, C74amJRp730)
Former design ethicist at Google; studied at Stanford's Persuasive Technology Lab. I read the whole transcript (about 17 minutes).

- **"A control room" that shapes a billion people's thoughts** [0:12–1:22](https://youtu.be/C74amJRp730?t=12). Notifications "schedule" thoughts you didn't intend to have.
- **The race for attention** [1:44–3:10](https://youtu.be/C74amJRp730?t=104). YouTube autoplays the next video, so Netflix autoplays the next episode, so Facebook autoplays everything. "Technology is not neutral". It becomes "this race to the bottom of the brain stem".
  - **Simulation link:** autoplay is a **default**, the strongest trick in the model. You must act to stop.
- **Snapstreaks: "something they don't want to lose"** [3:31–4:38](https://youtu.be/C74amJRp730?t=211). A counter of days in a row talking to a friend. Teens hand their passwords to friends on holiday to keep streaks alive.
  - **Psychology:** loss aversion (Kahneman & Tversky 1979) and the sunk-cost effect.
  - His key line: in the 1970s, gossiping on the phone, "there wasn't a hundred engineers on the other side of the screen who knew exactly how your psychology worked".
  - **Then vs now:** this sentence is the paper's "old tricks, new tools" thesis.
- **Outrage wins by default** [4:38–5:31](https://youtu.be/C74amJRp730?t=278). "We don't choose outrage. It happens to us". People want to share it. A feed choosing between an outrage version and a calm version would pick outrage, "not because someone consciously chose that, but because that worked better".
  - **Research link:** Brady et al. 2017 (moral-emotional words spread more) and Rathje et al. 2021, both in Report 4.
- **Targeting a lie at the most susceptible** [5:43–6:00](https://youtu.be/C74amJRp730?t=343). Ad-funded feeds let anyone who pays "precisely target a lie directly to the people who are most susceptible".
  - **Simulation link:** in v2, a trick hits gut deciders hardest (90% vs 84%). Targeting would aim it only at them.
- **His three fixes** [6:55–7:49](https://youtu.be/C74amJRp730?t=415):
  1. Accept that "we are persuadable": a "self-aware Enlightenment".
  2. Accountability: "the only form of ethical persuasion that exists is when the goals of the persuader are aligned with the goals of the persuadee".
  3. Design for "time well spent".
  - **Use in the paper:** his definition of ethical persuasion is the clearest line between persuasion and manipulation so far. **[YOUR OPINION]** Do you agree with it?
- **"Our biggest competitors are Facebook, YouTube and sleep"** [14:12](https://youtu.be/C74amJRp730?t=852). Harris attributes this to the CEO of Netflix.
- **Reflective brain vs lizard brain** [14:31–15:29](https://youtu.be/C74amJRp730?t=871). Chris Anderson asks: what if platforms asked you whether to optimise for "your reflective brain or your lizard brain"?
  - **Simulation link:** this is the model's "thinking" trait, and the "slow down" protection, which was the strongest one.
- **Two kinds of persuadability** [15:34–16:07](https://youtu.be/C74amJRp730?t=934): by argument, or "being persuaded without even knowing that you're thinking".
  - **Paper link:** this is the paper's definition of manipulation (Section 1.1).
- **Caution:** Harris is an advocate, and this talk gives examples rather than data. Pair it with the research it points to.

## 2. Robert Cialdini (with Steve Martin), "Science of Persuasion" (Influence at Work, cFdCzN7RYbw)
An 11-minute animated summary by the author of *Influence* (1984). I read the whole transcript. Cialdini lists six shortcuts (a seventh, "unity", was added in 2016).

| Principle | Study he cites | Number he gives | In our simulation / cases |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Reciprocity** [1:20](https://youtu.be/cFdCzN7RYbw?t=80) | Restaurant mints and tips | 1 mint: +3% tips; 2 mints: +14%; 1 mint plus "for you nice people, here's an extra mint": +23% | Not in the model. Cases: Caesar's will (PH #16), pharma gifts to doctors (Report 2) |
| **Scarcity** [3:06](https://youtu.be/cFdCzN7RYbw?t=186) | Concorde sales jumped the day after British Airways announced its end (2003) | (anecdote) | Scarcity trick, +11 |
| **Authority** [4:10](https://youtu.be/cFdCzN7RYbw?t=250) | Estate agents: receptionist mentions the expert's years of experience before connecting | +20% appointments, +15% contracts | Authority trick (v2), +11 |
| **Consistency** [6:04](https://youtu.be/cFdCzN7RYbw?t=364) | A small "drive safely" window card, then a big lawn sign (Freedman & Fraser 1966, foot-in-the-door) | 4× more agreed | Not in the model. Possible v3 idea |
| **Liking** [7:40](https://youtu.be/cFdCzN7RYbw?t=460) | MBA negotiators who first shared personal information and found a similarity | 55% → 90% reached agreement | Not in the model |
| **Consensus (social proof)** [9:05](https://youtu.be/cFdCzN7RYbw?t=545) | Hotel towel cards (Goldstein, Cialdini & Griskevicius 2008) | "75% of guests reuse": +26%; "75% of people *in this room*": +33% | Fake popularity, +6 |

**Key points for the paper:**
- **Why shortcuts exist** [0:38](https://youtu.be/cFdCzN7RYbw?t=38): "in the increasingly overloaded lives we lead… we need shortcuts". This is exactly Bernays's point (1928) that people can't check everything, and the simulation's "thinking time" idea.
- **"Not by what was given, but how it was given"** [2:51](https://youtu.be/cFdCzN7RYbw?t=171). Personal and unexpected beats bigger.
- **"Arrange for someone to do it for you"** [4:56](https://youtu.be/cFdCzN7RYbw?t=296). Authority works even when the introducer clearly benefits. This is Bernays's third-party technique again.
- **People deny it works on them** [10:37](https://youtu.be/cFdCzN7RYbw?t=637): most people think the sign "will have no influence on your behavior whatsoever", but it does. This is the "bias blind spot" (Pronin et al. 2002), and it explains why prebunking is weak.
- **Ethics:** Cialdini says to use these "in an entirely ethical way", by honestly pointing to real facts. Contrast with dark patterns, which use the same principles dishonestly (fake scarcity, fake reviews). Harris's test fits here: are the persuader's goals aligned with yours?

**Cautions:**
- This is a promotional video for Cialdini's consulting firm.
- The tip study numbers come from Strohmetz, Rind, Fisher & Lynn (2002), *Journal of Applied Social Psychology* ✔ (summaries agree on about 3% / 14% / 23%).
- Some social-proof field effects have replicated less strongly; for example, a German replication found towel messages helped, but the "75% of guests" norm message was *not* better than a standard message, and "guests in this room" effects were inconsistent (Bohner & Schlüter 2014, *PLOS ONE* ✔). Quote the numbers as "reported", not as fixed laws.

## 3. Jonathan Haidt, "The moral roots of liberals and conservatives" (TED 2008, vs41JrnGaxc)
A social psychologist (NYU, author of *The Righteous Mind*, 2012). I read the whole transcript (about 18 minutes). **This talk is good for balance:** it explains both left and right instead of mocking one.

- **Teams shut down open-minded thinking** [3:13–3:20](https://youtu.be/vs41JrnGaxc?t=193). "When people all share values… they become a team. And once you engage the psychology of teams, it shuts down open-minded thinking."
  - He shows his own mostly liberal audience mocking the other side after an election ([3:25–4:08](https://youtu.be/vs41JrnGaxc?t=205)), and says that's being "trapped in a moral Matrix".
  - **Simulation link:** echo chambers plus confirmation bias (v2). **Report link:** Le Bon's crowd, and WTF Ep 2's "find your tribe".
- **Five moral foundations** [5:37–7:43](https://youtu.be/vs41JrnGaxc?t=337): harm/care, fairness/reciprocity, in-group/loyalty, authority/respect, purity/sanctity.
  - From YourMorals.org surveys (about 23,000 Americans at the time): liberals rely mostly on the first two; conservatives on all five. He says the same pattern appears in many regions, including South Asia ([9:17](https://youtu.be/vs41JrnGaxc?t=557)).
  - **Research:** Graham, Haidt & Nosek 2009 (*JPSP*). Later work supports the left–right difference, but the five-foundation model has critics. Measurement issues; "liberty" was added as a sixth.
- **Why this matters for manipulation (my link):** a message works best when it's framed in the audience's own moral language. Feinberg & Willer (2015) showed that "moral reframing" (for example, environmental arguments framed as purity or patriotism for conservatives) changes minds more.
  - This is the **ethical** version of what the WTF Ep 4 guest predicted AI would do: find the words that fit inside someone's "immune system".
  - Manipulators also use it: fear messages aimed at loyalty and purity, and outrage messages aimed at harm.
- **"Sports is to war as pornography is to sex"** [6:52](https://youtu.be/vs41JrnGaxc?t=412). We create tribes for fun.
  - **Then vs now:** Jiang's "the World Cup is a substitute for war" (Report 4) and the Greek theatre festival (PH #9). Same drive, different stage.
- **Punishment makes groups cooperate** [11:32–12:48](https://youtu.be/vs41JrnGaxc?t=692). In Fehr & Gächter's public-goods game (2002, *Nature*), cooperation decays until people are allowed to punish free riders, then it rises. Even "shame or embarrassment or gossip" works.
  - **Then vs now:** the "religious shape" of group beliefs (Le Bon) and Constantine's orthodoxy (PH #26). Groups enforce belief by punishment. Online, the punishment is pile-ons.
- **Both sides have an insight** [14:09–15:08](https://youtu.be/vs41JrnGaxc?t=849). Liberals speak for the weak and want change "even at the risk of chaos"; conservatives see that "order is really hard to achieve… and really easy to lose" (quoting Burke).
  - He uses yin and yang, and Vishnu (preserver) and Shiva (destroyer) sharing one body ([15:26](https://youtu.be/vs41JrnGaxc?t=926)). An Indian example the user may like.
- **Moral humility as the defence** [17:19–17:53](https://youtu.be/vs41JrnGaxc?t=1039). "Everybody thinks they are right." To change people, first understand that, then "step out of the moral Matrix".
  - **Paper link:** a defence against manipulation that targets *you*, not the manipulator. Compare with "slow down" (the strongest protection in the simulation).

**Cautions:**
- 2008 talk, US-centred; the joke images reflect that time.
- He says the openness/liberal link "corresponds to reality". It's a real but moderate correlation, not a rule.

## 4. Sander van der Linden, "Stopping the spread of misinformation" (APA *Speaking of Psychology* podcast, EIFSofwEoOc)
A Cambridge psychologist who created the *Bad News* prebunking game (the Roozenbeek & van der Linden study in our benchmarks); author of *Foolproof* (2023). I read the keyword passages (149 of 373 lines).

- **How much misinformation do people see?** [5:16–6:12](https://youtu.be/EIFSofwEoOc?t=316). Anywhere from about 1% to 20% of a person's media diet, depending on whether you count only outright falsehoods or also half-truths, biased narratives and misleading arguments. People who get news mainly from social media see more.
- **Repetition and the continued influence effect** [11:57–13:00](https://youtu.be/EIFSofwEoOc?t=717). Repeated claims are processed faster and feel truer (illusory truth). Even after a correction, misinformation "integrates itself in your memory… it kind of makes friends".
  - **Simulation link:** this confirms the model's repetition curve and "fact-check removes only part of the effect".
  - **Then vs now:** Le Bon (1895) said the same thing.
- **Who is most vulnerable** [13:12](https://youtu.be/EIFSofwEoOc?t=792): people who are more politically extreme, spend a lot of time on social media, or "engage in less analytic or deliberative thinking". The last group matches the model's "gut deciders".
- **Misinformation spreads like a virus** [14:25–15:47](https://youtu.be/EIFSofwEoOc?t=865). Epidemic network models work for it. Sometimes you must be "sneezed on multiple times" before you accept and spread a falsehood.
  - **Simulation link:** the network part of the model works this way.
- **Prebunking = a weakened dose** [16:22–18:04](https://youtu.be/EIFSofwEoOc?t=982). Show a weakened example of the manipulation technique and take it apart, before people meet the real thing. "It's much harder to unring a bell", so this is better than debunking afterwards.
- **⭐ The key numbers for our simulation** [19:54–20:31](https://youtu.be/EIFSofwEoOc?t=1194):
  - In their lab social-media simulations, prebunking can boost resistance by "60 sort of percent".
  - In the real world, with a 30-second unskippable YouTube ad, "maybe we're boosting you by 10%… people have limited attention, they're in the wild, they're distracted".
  - "Would people buy a vaccine at 10% efficacy?" His answer: small effects across billions of people add up ("herd immunity").
  - **This strongly supports our simulation's result:** prebunking alone moved the stacked attack only from 87% to 85%. The model's 0.8 setting (tricks keep 80% strength) sits between his lab and field numbers. Put this in paper Section 5.5 as a check.
- **The technique list, not the "right answers"** [22:53–23:21](https://youtu.be/EIFSofwEoOc?t=1373). Education shouldn't tell people what to believe; it should teach general techniques: polarising false dichotomies ("if you're pro-Israel you're anti-Palestine" and the reverse), emotional manipulation and so on.
  - Other techniques named: trolling, scapegoating minorities, impersonation, conspiracy framing ([29:44–31:36](https://youtu.be/EIFSofwEoOc?t=1784)).
  - **Then vs now:** scapegoating is exactly what Report 4 Part D warns about in Predictive History's content.
- **Bots amplify both sides to sow chaos** [30:16–30:49](https://youtu.be/EIFSofwEoOc?t=1816). His game teaches it with "pineapple on pizza"; real trolls do it with "race, guns, abortion".
  - **Simulation link:** the opposite of our influencers. The goal isn't pushing A; it's maximising the split. A possible v3 idea.
- **AI cuts both ways** [24:31–27:32](https://youtu.be/EIFSofwEoOc?t=1471):
  - AI makes personality-targeted messages cheap to produce, where before "people had to manually craft these things". A fake image of Trump's arrest spread because people were "in a receptive state".
  - But AI can also write prebunks and spot manipulation techniques.
- **Practical tips** [28:41–29:20](https://youtu.be/EIFSofwEoOc?t=1721): limit the data you disclose, and use the chronological feed instead of algorithmic recommendations where platforms allow it.

**Cautions:**
- He consults for Google (said in the intro, [7:50](https://youtu.be/EIFSofwEoOc?t=470)), which runs prebunking ads. A possible conflict of interest.
- Some researchers criticise inoculation studies for measuring general scepticism (people rate *all* news as less reliable) rather than better judgement (Modirrousta-Galian & Higham 2023, *J. Experimental Psychology: General* ✔: reanalysing five studies, *Bad News* and *Go Viral!* made players say "false" more often to all news, without better discrimination). Include this criticism.
