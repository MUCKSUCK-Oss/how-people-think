# Predictive History: old-history videos, compared with today
Read on 6 Nov 2026 via the YouTube transcript panel. The user passed YouTube's check themselves, and I read keyword passages plus the key sections.

**Why these videos:** to compare how rulers steered people's beliefs and choices *then* with how it happens *now*.
**Reminder:** Jiang is a teacher giving his own interpretations. Where he states history, I've noted what historians generally agree on. His modern asides (for example about Trudeau) are opinions.

## 1. Civilization #16: Julius Caesar's Will and Octavian's Birth of Empire (Dc54IquR7ik)

**The will as a gift to the crowd** [1:47–2:35](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=107)
- Caesar's will gave every Roman citizen a cash gift (Jiang says three months of a soldier's wage) and turned his private gardens into public parks.
- The will also named a near-stranger, his 18-year-old great-nephew Octavian, as heir.
- Octavian *borrowed* money to pay the gift himself ([5:02](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=302)), so he looked like the faithful son.
- **Historians agree:** the will's cash gift to citizens and the gardens are recorded by ancient writers (Suetonius, Appian). The exact amount varies in the sources.
- **Then vs now:** reciprocity (Cialdini). A gift creates a feeling of owing something back, to the giver *and* to whoever honours the gift. Today's version: cash-transfer promises before elections, "free" product tiers, sponsor gifts to doctors (Report 2).
- **Le Bon used this exact case in 1895:** Antony moved the crowd not with rhetoric but "by reading his will to the multitude and pointing to his corpse". One vivid image beats argument.

**"His greatness was in his myth-making genius"** [16:02–18:44](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=962)
- Jiang's thesis: Caesar's myth (the man of destiny who would save the Republic) competed with Rome's older myth of Brutus the king-killer. Caesar's murder made *his* myth the dominant one. "If you want to create political change… you have to change the myths."
- His modern examples: Justin Trudeau, carried by love transferred from his father Pierre, and George W. Bush. (These are his opinions; calling Trudeau "the most incompetent" is his partisan aside.)
- **Then vs now:** brand inheritance and halo effects; political dynasties. India has many examples too, across parties.

**Martyrdom flips public opinion: the guilt effect** [29:00–30:59](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=1740)
- While Caesar was alive, Romans loved him but feared he wanted to be king. After the murder they felt guilt for doubting him ("if all they did was believe in Caesar… Caesar would still be alive"), and his generous will deepened that guilt.
- **Then vs now:** this is the same mechanism Jiang applies to Trump ("attacks make him a Messiah", Report 1, Part F) and that is often discussed after assassination attempts. Psychology: sympathy after attacks; reactance against attackers.
- **Research link:** studies of "sympathy effects" after attacks on candidates are mixed. Present this as a hypothesis.

**Personal loyalty bought with money** [39:20–40:24](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=2360)
- Octavian made Egypt his private estate and used its wealth to pay the professional army himself, and he gave conquered land to veterans. The army became loyal to him, not to the Senate.
- People accepted it because they believed he was driven by a mission to restore the Republic. Jiang: "because they believed him too much", the Republic was destroyed.
- **Historians agree:** Augustus did personally fund the soldiers' retirement scheme (the *aerarium militare*) and controlled Egypt as a personal province.
- **Then vs now:** patronage; leaders who make institutions loyal to a person. Le Bon: crowds give up freedom to a strong figure they worship.

**"A brilliant political manipulator"** [42:52](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=2572)
- Jiang calls Octavian (Augustus) not brilliant, not charismatic, not a great general, but someone who acted and balanced factions for 40 years. He kept the *forms* of the Republic while holding all the power.
- **Then vs now:** keeping the old labels while changing the substance is exactly what Le Bon noted (crowds change names, not institutions), and what Machiavelli advised (appear, don't be).

## 2. Civilization #17: Homer, Vergil, and the War for the Soul of Rome (myTurqU7NxU)
(Note: the auto-captions write "Trojans" as "children".)

**Augustus's three problems** [6:39–10:43](https://youtu.be/myTurqU7NxU?t=399)
1. **Legitimacy.** In the ancient world, the right to rule came from an old family. Augustus had to "construct a new myth": that his family descended from Aeneas, the Trojan founder of Rome.
2. **Identity.** Rome's old identity, "piety, liberty and the Republic", encouraged new Brutuses to kill the ruler. He needed one built on piety and **obedience**.
3. **Culture.** Greek culture (Homer, Plato, theatre) dominated, and Augustus thought it made Romans individualistic and selfish.

**Commissioning an epic to replace Homer in schools** [12:05–14:16](https://youtu.be/myTurqU7NxU?t=725)
- Roman children memorised the *Iliad*. Augustus asked Vergil, the greatest living poet, to write a Latin epic, the *Aeneid*, to replace Homer as the cornerstone of education.
- Jiang calls it "the greatest work of propaganda ever in human history". He says Homer was an educator and Vergil a propagandist ([15:40](https://youtu.be/myTurqU7NxU?t=940)).
- **Historians agree:** the *Aeneid* was written under Augustus's patronage (through his adviser Maecenas), it links Augustus's family to Aeneas, and it became a school text for centuries. Whether it is simple propaganda is debated; many scholars read real doubt and sadness in it. Present Jiang's view as one reading, as he himself says ([16:09](https://youtu.be/myTurqU7NxU?t=969), [28:21](https://youtu.be/myTurqU7NxU?t=1701)).

**The Trojan Horse as a warning about clever talk** [42:01–43:54](https://youtu.be/myTurqU7NxU?t=2521)
- In the *Aeneid*, a Greek wins over the Trojans with a false, moving, logical story, and they let the horse in. Jiang's reading: "the real Trojan horse is Greek culture": logic, philosophy and theatre used to manipulate and lie. The lesson: resist it.
- **"Oh" moment:** a propaganda poem that warns its readers *against persuasion*, so that they trust only the emperor's story. Compare with modern "don't trust the media, trust me" messaging, from any side.

**"The end point of history is Augustus"** [54:46–58:19](https://youtu.be/myTurqU7NxU?t=3286)
- The poem presents everything as leading to Augustus and the Pax Romana, eternal peace: "all you have to do is obey and the world will be perfect… history has stopped."
- **Then vs now:** "end of history" narratives that make the current order feel final and natural. Le Bon: crowd beliefs take a religious shape, with no discussion.

**Then vs now: the education channel**
- Augustus changed the *school text* to change a generation. Bernays (1928, ch. VIII and XI) wrote about getting schools and colleges on your side; Report 5 covers how the SAT shaped elite universities.
- **Big point for your paper:** the most lasting manipulation isn't an ad. It's what children memorise.

## 3. Civilization #12: The Tyranny of Alexander the Great (iTuRB48Of9Y)

**Jiang's model: a tyrant demands total obedience** [4:46–6:30](https://youtu.be/iTuRB48Of9Y?t=286)
- His prediction method: a leader who grew up under a powerful father's shadow becomes a tyrant who demands "complete and total obedience", treats any independent talent as disloyal, and never stops expanding ([51:53](https://youtu.be/iTuRB48Of9Y?t=3113)).
- **Caution:** this is Jiang's psychological theory applied backwards to Alexander's known life, so of course it "fits". Hindsight bias, as noted in Report 4 Part C.

**Fear as policy: destroying Thebes** [15:15–15:53](https://youtu.be/iTuRB48Of9Y?t=915)
- After Thebes revolted, Alexander destroyed the city (335 BC, well documented) to make the other Greek cities afraid.
- **Then vs now:** making an example of one rebel to deter others is still used, as in the "regime-change toolkit" and "fear causes obedience" (Report 4).

**Loyalty for pay vs loyalty for belonging** [26:12–28:17](https://youtu.be/iTuRB48Of9Y?t=1572)
- Jiang compares the two armies on cohesion and devotion. Macedonians shared one culture; Darius's Persian army was multicultural and loyal "because he pays them".
- **Then vs now:** shared identity holds people together more strongly than payment (social identity theory, Tajfel & Turner 1979).

**"Son of God" branding** [32:58–34:50](https://youtu.be/iTuRB48Of9Y?t=1978)
- At the Siwa oasis in Egypt, Alexander was greeted as son of Zeus-Ammon, the highest god of both Egyptians and Greeks combined. He began to claim his real father was a god, not Philip.
- **Historians agree:** the Siwa visit and the "son of Ammon" title are recorded by ancient sources (Arrian, Plutarch). Many historians see it as political as well as religious, a way to be accepted as pharaoh by Egyptians.
- **Then vs now:** one symbol that speaks to two audiences at once (Egyptian and Greek). Le Bon: the hero acclaimed by the crowd "is a veritable god for that crowd". Bernays: a symbol that works across groups.

**From persuasion to purges** [35:45–48:28](https://youtu.be/iTuRB48Of9Y?t=2145)
- As Alexander's claims grew, old loyal generals objected and were killed or sidelined (Philotas, Parmenion and Cleitus are documented). Macedonian officers were replaced with Persians "who are more loyal".
- **Then vs now:** replacing independent advisers with loyalists is a common warning sign in leaders of any country or company.

**Myths outlive the man** [51:11](https://youtu.be/iTuRB48Of9Y?t=3071)
- Later writers recast even Alexander's tyranny as "part of God's plan". Le Bon (Book I, ch. II) made the same point about Napoleon's legend changing within 50 years.

## 4. Civilization #26: Constantine's Monotheistic Revolution (za30rbC3tTg)

**The emperor's face everywhere: the imperial cult** [5:03](https://youtu.be/za30rbC3tTg?t=303)
- Roman rulers promoted the imperial cult: the emperor treated as divine, with "his pictures, his statues… everywhere including coins". Jiang compares it to a cult of personality.
- **Historians agree:** emperor worship and the use of coins and statues as mass media are well documented. A coin was the only "screen" that reached every market.
- **Then vs now:** a leader's face on billboards, currency, apps and government schemes. Repetition and familiarity (mere exposure, Zajonc 1968). Le Bon: altars then, portraits and statues now.

**How a small sect grew: recruit the patron, and his network follows** [13:36–16:47](https://youtu.be/za30rbC3tTg?t=816)
- Jiang argues Paul's branch of Christianity won because it copied Rome's patron–client structure. A local aristocrat became bishop, and "the local bishop would then convert his entire network".
- Only the bishop held the correct interpretation, so the church could scale.
- **Research link:** sociologist Rodney Stark (*The Rise of Christianity*, 1996) also argued that early Christianity spread through social networks, not mass preaching.
- **Then vs now:** the same "win the key men" method Bernays described in 1928, and the influencer-first growth a social-media founder described on WTF Ep 2.

**A new religion to unify an empire** [17:42–18:41](https://youtu.be/za30rbC3tTg?t=1062)
- "Great kings always want to introduce new religions to consolidate their… authority." Constantine backed Christianity to unify the empire.
- ⚠ **Correction:** Jiang says Constantine was "the first pope". That's wrong. The bishops of Rome existed before him, and he was never pope. He did call and preside over the Council of Nicaea in 325 ([23:32](https://youtu.be/za30rbC3tTg?t=1412)), which historians agree on.
- **Then vs now:** rulers using religion or a national ideology as glue. Le Bon: "Napoleon… by becoming a Catholic… ended the war."

**"Orthodoxy" = right thinking; disagreement becomes heresy** [3:39–3:57](https://youtu.be/za30rbC3tTg?t=219) and [18:41–19:15](https://youtu.be/za30rbC3tTg?t=1121)
- Once there's one official church, there's one "correct" belief, and other versions are pushed out.
- Jiang says that faith becomes "blind obedience… you have to memorize it" ([27:21](https://youtu.be/za30rbC3tTg?t=1641)), and that the church fought heretics through crusades and inquisitions ([51:07](https://youtu.be/za30rbC3tTg?t=3067)).
- **Caution:** this is a sweeping, one-sided picture. Many Christians and historians would describe faith and church history very differently. Use it as one argument, not a fact.
- **Then vs now:** any group that labels disagreement as betrayal ("heretic", "anti-national", "traitor") makes discussion impossible. Le Bon's "religious shape" of convictions: no discussion, and outsiders are enemies.

**Other claims to flag (opinions, not facts)**
- "Positive psychology… the dumbest idea in the world" ([41:08](https://youtu.be/za30rbC3tTg?t=2468)): his opinion. Positive psychology is a real research field with mixed results; it isn't simply "think you're happy and you are".
- That the Church banned the Book of Enoch so ordinary people wouldn't question the powerful ([44:35](https://youtu.be/za30rbC3tTg?t=2675)): an oversimplification. Enoch was left out of most Christian canons for theological reasons, and it's still in the Ethiopian Orthodox Bible.

## 5. Civilization #9: Aeschylus, Sophocles, and Euripides as Prophets of Democracy (96hzWlozdHw)
(Note: the auto-captions write "Trojan" as "children" and "Thebes" as "thieves".)

**Three institutions that build a collective identity** [0:14–2:03](https://youtu.be/96hzWlozdHw?t=14)
- Jiang: every society shapes how its people think through **public schools, mass media/entertainment and religion or ritual**. America builds an individualist identity; China a collectivist one.
- In Athens, **theatre** did this job. Its main function was to create the identity of a "democratic citizen" ([2:16](https://youtu.be/96hzWlozdHw?t=136)).

**Theatre as a civic machine** [2:59–6:43](https://youtu.be/96hzWlozdHw?t=179)
- Plays ran at the festival of Dionysus. Rich families paid for productions to win the people's favour. Winners were chosen by vote, actors were ordinary citizens, and theatres held about 10,000 people (some estimates say more).
- Everyone knew the myths and the lines; the point was taking part together ([7:57](https://youtu.be/96hzWlozdHw?t=477)).
- **Historians agree:** the Dionysia was a state festival; wealthy citizens funded choruses as a public duty (the *choregia*), which also bought them popularity. Theatre as civic education is a standard view (Goldhill and others).
- **Then vs now:** rich patrons funding the entertainment everyone watches. Compare with Bernays (1928, ch. XI): the motion picture as "the greatest unconscious carrier of propaganda". Also Report 4 (Bollywood, the Hollywood–Pentagon link).

**Aeschylus: "the gods gave you democracy; vote seriously"** [17:03–20:18](https://youtu.be/96hzWlozdHw?t=1023)
- In the *Eumenides*, Orestes is tried by a citizen jury; when the votes tie, Athena casts the deciding vote.
- Jiang's reading: the myth tells Athenians that democracy is a divine gift to honour.
- **Then vs now:** grounding a political system in a sacred origin story, as with founding-father myths and constitutions treated as near-sacred.

**Sophocles: "should the king listen to the mob?"** [28:14–30:36](https://youtu.be/96hzWlozdHw?t=1694)
- In *Antigone*, Creon's son tells him a ruler must listen to the people. Jiang reads the play as anti-king.

**Euripides: art that criticises its own audience** [33:02–36:17](https://youtu.be/96hzWlozdHw?t=1982) and [46:02–46:30](https://youtu.be/96hzWlozdHw?t=2762)
- In 415 BC, a year after Athens massacred the men of Melos, Euripides staged *The Trojan Women*, a play about the suffering of the defeated, which "made everyone in Athens weep". Jiang: it told Athenians "we are a terrible people". It lost the competition.
- **Historians agree:** the Melos massacre (416 BC, described by Thucydides) and *The Trojan Women* (415 BC) are real. Scholars debate whether the play directly targets Melos.
- Jiang's point: real democracy needs "argumentation, debate and self-reflection", including art that tells the public uncomfortable truths. The crowd usually punishes it.
- **Then vs now:** the opposite of rally-round-the-flag (Report 4). The crowd rewards flattery and punishes self-criticism (Le Bon); the same thing happens to unpopular truths today.

**Pericles: democracy → empire → war** [43:37–45:18](https://youtu.be/96hzWlozdHw?t=2617)
- Pericles' funeral speech (in Thucydides) praised Athens as tolerant and open, then concluded that the young should fight for the empire.
- **Then vs now:** using a society's best values ("we are free and open") to justify war. Jiang notes that everyone was in the audience.

**The Bacchae as satire on mass entertainment** [48:54–49:29](https://youtu.be/96hzWlozdHw?t=2934)
- One reading: Euripides mocks the Dionysus festival, and by extension the crowd, as a "wild party that's trying to please everyone" rather than reflection.
- **Then vs now:** the debate about whether entertainment platforms educate citizens or just please them (WTF Ep 2: the "dopamine hit at both ends").
