# Literature review notes v1 (draft for you to rewrite)

These are notes to study and restate in your own words, not text to paste. Every claim is traceable to 04_bibliography.

## 1. Persuasion is usually weak
Large field and survey experiments find small or near-zero average effects of campaign contact and advertising (Kalla & Broockman 2018; Coppock, Hill & Vavreck 2020). Mercier (2017) reaches the same conclusion from a broader psychology review: people check messages against prior beliefs and source trust. Chong & Druckman (2007) add that when competing frames exist, effects shrink, though they don't vanish. DellaVigna & Gentzkow (2010) survey the same question across consumers, voters, donors and investors.

## 2. Persuasion can be strong under special conditions
Where one source dominates information and the message fits listeners' priors, effects look large: Nazi radio after the takeover of broadcasting (Adena et al. 2015), RTLM in Rwanda (Yanagizawa-Drott), and Russian TV in Ukraine (Peisakhin & Rozenas 2018). The same studies show backfire or no effect where priors point the other way.

## 3. Disagreement and how to reconcile it
The two groups look contradictory but measure different settings. The first studies are competitive environments with a mild treatment; the second are information monopolies or near-monopolies. Reconciliation hypothesis (yours to test or argue): *effects scale with how little competing information the audience gets and how well the message fits what they already believe.* Caution: the strong-effect studies are quasi-experimental (using geography), the weak-effect studies are randomized. Different designs can differ for reasons other than the setting.

## 4. Attention, not belief
A separate strand says the goal is not to change minds but to shift attention. King, Pan & Roberts (2017) and Roberts (2020) find the Chinese state floods rather than argues. Guriev & Treisman (2022) describe a broader style of "spin" rule. Lewandowsky et al. (2020) and a 2025 preprint test whether a leader's posts shift media coverage in a democracy, with associations and caveats about intent and outlet choice. Evidence here is thinner and more contested.

## 5. Repetition and truth
Lab work finds repeated statements feel truer (Dechêne et al. 2010, medium effect; a 2026 meta-analysis reportedly finds a smaller effect after bias adjustment). RAND's firehose model builds on this (Paul & Matthews 2016). Gap: the jump from "feels truer in a lab" to "changes votes or behavior" is unproven.

## 6. Frameworks
Wardle & Derakhshan (2017) give vocabulary (mis-/dis-/mal-information; agent, message, interpreter). Oreskes & Conway (2010) document manufactured doubt as a historical case.
