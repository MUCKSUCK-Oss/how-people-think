## The 5 most useful findings
1. **China's spy agency made its first feature film in 2026.** The MSS backed *Scare Out* (directed by Zhang Yimou) to promote "national security education", with officers on set throughout. Its aim goes beyond pride: it pushes viewers to **report suspicious people**. This is new and not in the paper yet (RESEARCH.md F3).
2. **The KGB did it in 1973, and the FSB still gives prizes.** KGB chief Andropov backed *Seventeen Moments of Spring*, and the KGB's deputy head was its consultant under a false name. Since 2006 the FSB has given yearly awards to films that show it well. Russia's newer war films still flop (*The Witness*: about $70k earned on about $2M). Money buys the film, not the audience (F2).
3. **The Nazi radio study is the best "then vs now" evidence.** Adena et al. (2015, QJE): radio helped the Nazis most where antisemitism was already high and *backfired* where it was low. China's film experiment (Yao 2024) found the same. Propaganda amplifies what people already lean towards, which matches your simulation results (past-rulers notes, case 11).
4. **Ashoka edited his own message by region.** The famous edict expressing remorse for Kalinga was **left out in Kalinga itself**; local officials got instructions on order instead. It is a 2,200-year-old example of targeted messaging (case 3). (Check this against a scholarly edition before using it.)
5. **On *Dhurandhar*, check facts and framing separately.** Dhruv Rathee's checkable facts mostly held up (11 Pulwama warnings, the De La Rue case, Doval's "deepfake" clip being genuine), but his framing was heavily loaded (Nazi comparison, name-calling). Sanjay Dixit's framing was loaded **and** his two big claims were false. Abhijit Chavda praised the film while saying he hadn't seen it. Mohak Mangal stayed "centre" by talking only about violence. I found **no evidence the government funded the film**; the issue is blending fact and fiction, plus rewards like Ladakh's tax exemption.

## Ideas for a second movies test (for you to decide; nothing was run)
1. **"Inspired by real events" vs "fictional" vs no label** on the same spy film. This tests the *Dhurandhar* problem directly: does "inspired by real events" make invented scenes (e.g. a minister helping the enemy) feel true?
2. **Mirror test:** show the same plot with the parties swapped (the Rathee "Bhavandar" idea). Do proud or critical viewers judge "propaganda" differently depending on which side is the villain? That would be a clean test of partisan bias.
3. **Agency type:** the same hero story credited to the army, a spy agency or the police, with or without a "made with the help of…" notice. Do intelligence agencies gain or lose more from disclosure than armies?
4. **"Report suspicious people" ending** (the MSS model): does a spy film that ends with "if you see something, report it" raise willingness to report neighbours, and does a sponsor notice cancel that?
5. **Fix from round 1:** the "does this feel like propaganda?" question hit 100% in every version. Use a 1–7 scale or a forced choice between two films instead.
