# Movies and propaganda: research before simulating
*Collected 15 Nov 2026, BEFORE any simulation design. Sources are web-search summaries of papers, reports and news [summary]. I didn't read the full papers. Items marked [CHECK] are disputed, come mostly from one side, or are unconfirmed.*

**The question:** can a film change what people believe or choose, even when they know it's "just a movie"? And who pays to make films say certain things?

**Scope:** global, not one country. Cases come from the USA, China, Russia, India, Pakistan, Turkey and Nazi Germany.

**Balance rule:** governments and studios of every political colour use film. The list includes right-leaning cases (Pentagon films, Indian Hindu-nationalist films) and left-leaning ones (*Fahrenheit 9/11*, *An Inconvenient Truth*, *Will & Grace*). Anti-government films (*JFK*) are included too. The point is the *mechanism*, not which side is worse.

---

## 1. Who pays for the message: real cases

### USA: the Pentagon's script deal
- **How it works:** the Defense Department has an entertainment liaison office in Los Angeles. Filmmakers who want jets, ships, bases or soldiers as extras send in the **whole script**. The military asks for changes; if the film refuses, it gets no help, which makes it much more expensive.
  - Since 2013, formal support has required sending both the script and the final cut for approval (Washington Times, 2013) [summary].
- **How many films:** Tom Secker and Matthew Alford got Pentagon and CIA documents through freedom-of-information requests. Their count is **over 1,800 films and TV shows**. Other write-ups say 1,000 films + 1,000 TV shows, or over 2,500. [CHECK: the numbers vary with scope and date, and nobody independent has checked them.] The authors are openly critical of the military, so their counts are worth checking.
- **Help refused (reported):**
  - *Platoon* (1986) and *Born on the Fourth of July* (1989): showed US soldiers committing crimes.
  - *Independence Day* (1996): the military looked helpless.
  - *Windtalkers*: generals looked too warlike; *Man of Steel* was first refused as too cartoonish [summary; CHECK].
- **Help given:** *Top Gun*, two *Iron Man* films (after script changes) and many others.
- **New rule (2023):** the Pentagon won't help films changed to please Chinese censors (after Sen. Ted Cruz's SCRIPT Act). A June 2026 Federal Register note says producers must now sign a form proving this [summary; CHECK the Register itself].
- **Why it matters for the paper:** the audience **never sees** the deal. A film looks like independent entertainment, but its view of the military was negotiated. This is the "hidden sender" problem from the secret-agencies topic (6.8) again.

### USA: Top Gun and recruiting, a myth and a smaller truth
- **The myth:** "Navy recruitment rose 500% after *Top Gun*." No source gives evidence for this. It's a "zombie statistic" that keeps being repeated [summary].
- **What's documented:** recruiters really did set up desks outside some cinemas. Secondary sources citing Navy Recruiting Command say enlistments rose about **8%** in 1986, but the Navy's ad budget also rose, so the film's own share is unclear [summary; CHECK the primary Navy data].
- **Captain Marvel (2019):** the Air Force ran recruiting ads before the film in **over 3,600 cinemas**, aimed especially at women. The Air Force Academy then had its most female applicants in 5 years, but the reporting itself says the cause is unclear [summary].
- **Lesson:** the popular story overstates film power; the real effect is probably real but small, and hard to separate from advertising.

### USA: Hollywood changing films for China
- **PEN America, 2020** ("Made in Hollywood, Censored by Beijing"): studios cut or changed scenes about Tibet, Taiwan, Hong Kong and LGBTQ characters to get into the Chinese market, sometimes without even being asked.
- **Example:** in the 2019 *Top Gun: Maverick* trailer, the Taiwan and Japan flags on the jacket were swapped for made-up patches (a Chinese company, Tencent, was an early investor). The flags were back in the 2022 film. The producers never explained [summary].
- **Lesson:** propaganda can work by **leaving things out**, and here the pressure came from a foreign market, not the home government.

### China: state-backed blockbusters
- *Wolf Warrior 2* (2017) and *The Battle at Lake Changjin* (2021) are China's two highest-earning films ever (about 5.7 billion yuan each).
- *Lake Changjin* was government-sponsored and released on the eve of National Day. It shows Chinese soldiers defeating US troops in the Korean War [summary].
- **Lesson:** state films can be truly popular when they're made like real blockbusters and fit a mood people already have (rising national pride).

### Russia: state-funded war films that often flop
- The Ministry of Culture set funding priorities like "the heroism of Russian warriors" in Ukraine and reportedly set aside about **$395 million** in one round for films on the war, "Nazism" and similar themes (AP, Al Jazeera, 2023) [summary].
- *The Witness* (2023), the first feature about the war, was backed by the culture and defence ministries and premiered with little attention.
- Several such films were box-office failures (Al Jazeera: "box office bombs") [summary; CHECK: most sources are Western or Ukrainian].
- **Lesson:** money and state support don't guarantee an audience. The China–Russia contrast is useful: the same strategy is a hit in one place and a flop in another.

### India: the "propaganda film" debate
- *Uri: The Surgical Strike* (2019), *The Kashmir Files* (2022) and *The Kerala Story* (2023) were made **tax-free in several BJP-ruled states**, which is a direct government reward for a film.
  - Prime Minister Modi praised *The Kerala Story* in a campaign speech.
  - Its central claim (32,000 women recruited to ISIS) is widely disputed. The film itself later called it "inspired by true stories" [summary; CHECK the exact wording].
  - *The Kashmir Files* was a big hit (about 22× its budget, per one non-peer-reviewed analysis). Reports say some screenings had audiences shouting against Muslims [summary].
- **Balance:** many films with the same message flopped. Critics say "propaganda" is now a genre in India, while supporters call these films stories the older film industry ignored [summary].
- **No study found** that measures their effect on votes.
- **Balance (other side):** opposition-friendly films exist too (for example, films about the 2002 Gujarat riots), but none got state tax breaks. I didn't find a Congress-era tax-free equivalent [CHECK; worth searching more].

### Pakistan: the army as producer
- The military's media wing (**ISPR**) has made or funded dramas, songs and films since the 1990s: the film *Waar* (2013) and the drama *Ehd-e-Wafa* (2019–20).
- A 2024 Cambridge paper says these are made with private TV partners and are more complicated than "simple propaganda", but critics say they glorify the army [summary].

### Turkey: a TV series as soft power
- *Resurrection: Ertuğrul*, an Ottoman-era drama from Turkish state TV (TRT), was shown free in Pakistan on state TV at **Prime Minister Imran Khan's request**.
- It reportedly reached over 130 million viewers there [summary; CHECK the number], and Pakistan made up about 25% of its YouTube audience.
- Its producer is a member of Erdoğan's party.
- **Lesson:** a government can send a film or series *abroad* to shape how another country sees it.

### History: Nazi Germany and *The Birth of a Nation*
- *Jud Süß* (1940), an antisemitic drama, was a box-office success and was shown to SS units and the Hitler Youth [summary]. **Content warning:** this film is hate propaganda. It's mentioned here only as a historical case.
- **The Birth of a Nation (1915), the strongest causal study found:** Desmond Ang (*American Economic Review*, 2023) used the film's touring schedule. In the month after the film arrived in a county, **lynchings were about 4 times as likely**, race riots rose too, and Ku Klux Klan support grew later.
  - A critic (Kaestner, *Econ Journal Watch*, 2023) says the analysis is flawed [CHECK both].

## 2. Do films actually change minds? The studies

| Study | What was tested | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| **Hovland, Lumsdaine & Sheffield 1949**, "Why We Fight" (US Army) | WWII soldiers watched official orientation films (e.g. *The Battle of Britain*) | **Big gains in facts, smaller changes in opinions, almost none in motivation to fight.** The first real experiments on propaganda films |
| **Butler, Koopman & Zimbardo 1995** (*Political Psychology*) | Oliver Stone's *JFK*: about 50 people asked before vs about 50 after | After the film, people were **angrier, believed more in a conspiracy, and said they were less likely to vote**. Not a randomised test |
| **A 2007 study in *Political Communication*** (authors not confirmed) | *Fahrenheit 9/11* (anti-Bush, 2004): viewers vs people who *planned* to see it | Viewers were more negative about Bush even after controls. But polls said **about 78% of the audience were already Democrats**, and 78% said it wouldn't change their vote: mostly preaching to the choir [summary; CHECK the authors] |
| **Jacobsen 2011** (*J. Environmental Economics & Management*) | *An Inconvenient Truth* (2006): carbon-offset purchases near cinemas showing it | About **50% more offsets bought** nearby in the 2 months after, **back to normal within a year**. The effect was a bit stronger in conservative areas [summary] |
| **Kearns & Young 2017** (*Crime & Delinquency*) | 150 people randomly shown *24* clips: torture works / torture fails / no torture | Showing torture **working raised support** for torture. Showing it **failing didn't lower it** [summary] |
| **Pautz 2015** (*PS: Political Science & Politics*) | 69 students watched *Argo* or *Zero Dark Thirty* | About **25% changed their view of the government**, mostly for the better. Small, exploratory |
| **Schiappa, Gregg & Hewes 2005, 2006** | Shows with gay characters (*Six Feet Under*, *Queer Eye*, *Will & Grace*) | Watching was linked to **less prejudice**, most for viewers with no gay people in their lives ("parasocial contact"). The 2006 study is a survey, so it can't prove cause |
| **Paluck 2009** (*JPSP*), Rwanda field experiment | A year of a reconciliation radio drama vs a health drama, in 12 communities | It changed **what people thought others accepted** (norms) and how they behaved, but **barely changed their personal beliefs** |
| **Green & Brock 2000** (*JPSP*) | A story labelled as fact vs as fiction | Being "carried away" by a story, and the belief change that followed, were **generally the same with either label** |
| **Appel & Maleckar 2012**; **Ebert 2014** | "Fiction" vs "non-fiction" vs "fake" labels | The story persuaded in all three conditions. A "fiction" label didn't protect people; only "fake" weakened it a bit. Emotions were about the same for "true story" and fiction [summary] |
| **Braddock & Dillard 2016** (meta-analysis, 74 studies) | Stories vs no story | Stories shift beliefs and attitudes by a **small but real amount (r ≈ .17–.19)** [summary; CHECK the original] |

## 3. What the evidence says, in plain words
1. **Films change facts and feelings more easily than actions.** This held from Hovland (1949) to *An Inconvenient Truth* (2006), where behaviour faded within a year. It matches the earlier topics: information changes what people know more than what they do.
2. **"It's only fiction" doesn't protect you.** People absorb story messages almost as much when they know it's made up (Green & Brock; Appel). This is the opposite of the "labels" finding in the secret-agencies topic, where naming the source undid the effect.
3. **Films mostly reach people who already agree** (*Fahrenheit 9/11*: 78% Democrats). India's state tax breaks and China's National Day timing aim for the same "home crowd".
4. **Showing something work is stronger than showing it fail** (the *24* torture study). If heroes break the rules and win, viewers learn that the rule-breaking works.
5. **The strongest effects come where viewers have no real-life experience** (parasocial contact: people who knew no gay people; the military for civilians). Films fill the gap.
6. **The hidden sender:** the Pentagon deal, ISPR and Chinese market pressure are mostly invisible to viewers. Nobody has tested whether **telling viewers "the military approved this script"** changes how they react. **That gap is what our simulation can test.**
7. **Money isn't enough:** Russia's funded films flopped, while China's were hits. A film has to work *as a film* first.
8. **The biggest claims are often myths** (the "500% Top Gun recruits" figure). That's useful for the paper: people believe in the power of film more than the evidence shows.

## 4. What this means for the simulation design (for Maulik to decide)
- **The core test:** AI people read a short summary of an invented film (no real film or country is named), then answer questions about joining the army or trusting the military, or about the film's issue.
- **Possible versions:**
  1. **No film** (baseline).
  2. **A heroic film:** the army as brave, honest heroes.
  3. **A critical film:** soldiers who commit abuses (like *Platoon*).
  4. **The heroic film + a disclosure:** "this film's script was approved by the defence ministry in exchange for equipment." This tests the gap in point 6.
  5. **The heroic film + a "fiction" disclaimer** (tests Green & Brock).
  6. **A "rule-breaking works" film** (the hero tortures a suspect and saves the city), to test the *24* finding.
- **Who:** young people aged 17–24 from several countries (they're the recruiting target), with different views of their own military (proud, neutral, critical).
- **Answers:** interest in joining (1–7), trust in the military (1–7), "did the film feel like propaganda?" (yes/no), and a reason.
- **Known risk:** AI people only read a *summary*, not 2 hours of music, faces and action. **That makes the film much weaker than a real one**, so any effect we find is probably a lower bound. This is the biggest limit and must be stated in the paper.
