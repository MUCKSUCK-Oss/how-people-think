Part A. Why Trump wins: how supporters and observers explain it

1. "The most effective political athlete we've ever seen" (Chamath)

26:40–33:00 · heard it

  • The argument:
    • COVID broke trust in experts, so America needed a "vessel" for change.
    • Trump is unbeatable at connecting one-on-one, and he's "disarming".
    • His business background makes him a negotiator career politicians can't match.
    • America "needed somebody willing to break the norms".
  • Nikhil's reaction: he pushes back, but first says he's not sure how far he can argue "without getting into trouble".
  • Research:
    • The authentic rule-breaker: when voters think the system is illegitimate, a norm-breaking or even lying candidate reads as authentic. Hahl, Kim & Zuckerman Sivan 2018, ASR (paper, not re-read).
    • Warmth and competence: people judge others on warmth first, competence second. Fiske, Cuddy & Glick 2007 (paper, not re-read).
  • Balance: Chamath is an open Trump supporter.

2. The businessman-in-politics trend (WTF asks three leaders)

  • Christopher Luxon, NZ prime minister and a former CEO:
    • Nikhil compares him to Trump, DOGE and "less DEI" (6:39, 23:05).
    • Asked for one thing he likes about Trump and one he doesn't, Luxon refuses: "not for me to comment" (26:10).
  • Nikos Christodoulides, president of Cyprus:
    • Nikhil asks to see him as "the CEO of a country". He replies, "I'm not a CEO. I was never…" (1:00:31).
  • The contrast: Chamath praises openly, Luxon keeps quiet, and Christodoulides rejects the CEO frame. Same question, three answers.
  • The number from your earlier session: a 2017 Marketplace-Edison poll found 78% of Republicans and 32% of Democrats wanted government run more like a business (verify).

3. Same anger, two costumes: Trump and Mamdani (Ruchir Sharma)

24:45 · heard it

4. "The empire needs a scapegoat" (Jiang, Predictive History)

1:01:33 and 1:03:37 · heard it · his theory

  • Jiang says American power is in decline, so the elite need someone willing to be the openly aggressive face of it, so that all the blame lands on one person.

  • At 1:34:33 he adds that "everyone's attention is focused on Trump", so the bigger structural forces go unseen.
  • Research: this is a testable idea about personalization. Blaming individuals instead of situations is the fundamental attribution error (Ross 1977, paper, not re-read).
  • My note: whether a hidden "empire" picked Trump is Jiang's speculation. The attention part is the useful bit.

Part A. How drug companies sell when advertising is banned

1. The logo held on screen for an extra second (Mankind)

11:14 · heard it

  • India doesn't allow prescription drugs to be advertised to the public.
  • So Mankind launched consumer (over-the-counter) products and ran ads for those, keeping the Mankind logo on screen for more than a second instead of the usual half-second.
  • The aim was to make the company name familiar across India before its medicines reached each region.
  • Research: seeing a name repeatedly makes people like it more, the mere-exposure effect (Zajonc 1968, paper, not re-read).

2. 17,000 sales reps

43:44 · heard it

  • A guest: out of every ₹100 a drug sells for, it costs roughly ₹25–35 to make. Much of the rest pays for a sales force of about 17,000 medical reps who visit doctors.
  • Your takeaway: in India, pharma marketing happens person to person, aimed at doctors rather than patients.

3. "Our industry is ethical"

2:07:55 · heard it

  • A guest says the top 10 Indian pharma companies "don't do anything wrong".
  • Counterpoint: in the 2022 Dolo-650 case, tax officials alleged that the maker spent around ₹1,000 crore on freebies for doctors. The company denied it (verify).
  • India introduced a marketing code for pharma companies (UCPMP) in 2024 (verify).
  • Research: gifts create a feeling of owing something back, reciprocity (Cialdini 1984). Studies link pharma payments to doctors with more prescribing of the paying company's drugs (DeJong et al. 2016, JAMA Internal Medicine, paper, not re-read).

4. "Every superhit drug gets abused"

2:00:32 · heard it

  • On GLP-1 weight-loss drugs (Ozempic type): "whenever a product becomes superhit, it is being abused", and patients pressure doctors into prescribing them.
  • At 2:20:03, Nikhil asks whether a 25-year-old could build a GLP-1 brand the way people build protein brands. The answer: no, because it's prescription-only and can't be advertised.

5. "National security" as a sales line

1:45:48 · heard it

  • One guest dismisses the idea that China controlling India's drug ingredients is a security risk: "a narrative I don't believe in".
  • He adds that during COVID, Chinese suppliers were more reliable than Indian ones.
  • Your takeaway: industries borrow security language to win protection. Ask who benefits when you hear it.

7. Cuts to research funding

1:08:49 · heard it

  • A guest says Trump has cut a lot of public research funding.
  • He quotes the National Cancer Institute at $40B a year. That looks wrong: it's closer to the whole NIH budget, and the NCI's share is a few billion (verify).

Verify all of these:

  • 30 Sept 2025: Trump announced a Pfizer deal with some price cuts and a "TrumpRx" website.
  • What Pfizer got: a three-year break from planned tariffs.

Part D. Health claims from Predictive History (flagged)

  • COVID vaccines:
    • Jiang uses vaccine mandates as his example of "reason replaced by rules" (24:34).
    • At 2:40:21 he says the vaccines were "not tested" and "rushed", and that Sweden handled COVID best.

Part E. New in version 2 (Predictive History)

1. MK-Ultra: a real case of mind-control research

44:00 · heard it

  • Jiang describes the CIA's MK-Ultra programme: LSD and "brainwashing" experiments on people who didn't know, run by chemist Sidney Gottlieb, with most records destroyed.
  • This one is real history. MK-Ultra ran from about 1953 to 1973. In 1973 the CIA ordered most of its files destroyed. The surviving papers came out through the 1975 Church Committee and a 1977 Senate hearing (verify exact dates).
  • The "oh" moment: real abuses like this are why later, false conspiracy theories feel believable.

2. "I've never met anyone helped by a psychologist"

47:00 · heard it

  • This is one man's anecdote.
  • Research: meta-analyses find psychotherapy has a sizeable average effect (Smith & Glass 1977, American Psychologist; Wampold & Imel, The Great Psychotherapy Debate, 2015; paper and book, not re-read).
  • A good example of personal experience being used to dismiss a whole field.

3. The opium trade as a model

26:00 and 32:00 · heard it

  • Jiang: Britain sold opium to China with the help of local elites, and "the game is still being played today".
  • Real history: the Opium Wars (1839–42 and 1856–60), fought by Britain to keep the opium trade open.
  • His add-on: that the same game runs today is his theory.

4. COVID vaccines and "science as religion" (⚠ flagged)

4:46 · heard it

  • He calls science "the dominant religion" and uses COVID vaccines as his example.
  • This repeats the vaccine scepticism flagged in Part D. Large trials and real-world data showed the vaccines cut severe illness and death (paper, not re-read).

Part F. New in version 3

  • Semmelweis and the maternity clinics (44:00, heard it): two maternity clinics in the same hospital had very different death rates. Real history: Ignaz Semmelweis (1847) showed doctors' unwashed hands were killing mothers, and colleagues rejected him. A classic story of experts resisting evidence that blames them. It's also why the "Semmelweis reflex" became a phrase.
  • "Drugs" as a game-theory trap (version 2, Report 2, Part E, item 3) is his main pharma theme. Predictive History has almost no real pharma content, so for pharma rely on the WTF episodes.

How big companies and powerful people think

Read status, 5 Nov 2026: WTF passages from 41 episodes, and Predictive History passages from 111 videos.

Tags: "heard it" means I read that passage myself. "Paper, not re-read" means a study cited from memory. "Verify" means not checked yet.

Part A. In their own words (WTF guests)

1. "Your moat becomes distribution"

Nikesh Arora, CEO of Palo Alto Networks: 1:02:32 · heard it

  • In consumer businesses, over time the moat becomes distribution: "your product sometimes doesn't stay as good, but you still own distribution."
  • Without a good product, even those companies eventually fade.
  • At 25:09: "the big will become bigger" because they hold the data.
  • Your takeaway: the incumbent's playbook is to own the shelf, the app store or the habit, not necessarily the best product.

2. "Where does choice end and monopoly start?" (PhonePe)

1:22:56 · heard it

  • PhonePe, which holds a huge share of UPI payments, argues against a 30% market-share cap.
  • Its case: they don't control banks, routing or pricing, so customers choosing them isn't a monopoly.
  • The psychology: a dominant company reframes its dominance as consumer choice. That's framing (Tversky & Kahneman 1981).

3. "I wouldn't call it a monopoly"

0:00:06 · heard it

  • Nikhil: "Why does CATL have a monopoly on batteries?"
  • The guest: "I wouldn't call it a monopoly. I'd say they are very competitive."
  • Your takeaway: the wording changes how the same fact feels.

4. "Money warps every relationship"

Bryan Johnson: 23:49 · heard it

  • After making about $300M, every relationship has three people in it: "you, your money and the person".
  • His example: a $10 gift for your niece now looks stingy.
  • Jiang says something similar (1:13:47): power and love contradict each other, because "if you're a billionaire everyone's nice to you, so who truly loves you?"
  • Research: power lowers inhibition and empathy for others (Keltner, Gruenfeld & Anderson 2003, Psych Review, paper, not re-read).

5. "That's a stupid label"

Chamath: 23:07 · heard it

  • A commenter asks why, as a billionaire, he bothers building a product. Chamath: "first of all that's a stupid label… this is not how I measure myself."

6. "Billionaires on paper"

Omar Al Olama, UAE minister: 2:39 · heard it

  • Nikhil: tech billionaires like Sam Altman and Elon Musk "have virtual money, you have real money". Al Olama: "many billionaires are billionaires on paper."

7. Investors have two emotions

Vinod Khosla: 1:13:12 · heard it

  • Investors have "only two emotions, fear and greed", so every opportunity-rich space gets a bubble.
  • Nikhil adds Kahneman's point that people fear losses about twice as much as they enjoy gains (1:06:59).
  • Research: loss aversion, Kahneman & Tversky 1979 (paper, not re-read).

Part C. How a YouTube prophet persuades: Predictive History as a case study
